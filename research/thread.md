## Beats

**1. Gwinnett had the smallest share of rentals within reach of any county in
the country outside college towns.**
- In 2020-24, 10.5% of Gwinnett's rentals were within reach of its median
  renter household. That is the lowest of 1,922 counties with at least 1,000
  rentals, leaving out college towns.
- Caveats:
  - With college towns included, it ranks 3rd of 1,987.
  - Clayton County, next door, is at 11.0%, close enough that the two could
    swap places.
  - The ranking uses the tool's default settings (the median renter household,
    after taxes, 30% of income). Other settings give other rankings.
  - Counties with under 1,000 rentals, or marked low reliability, are left out.
- Three of the lowest four are Atlanta-area counties (Gwinnett, Clayton,
  DeKalb).
- Chart: `10-least-affordable.png`.

**2. The share fell from 25.5% in 2005-09 to 10.5% in 2020-24, a 59% decline.**
- The number of rentals grew 63%, but the number within reach fell 33%.
- The nation stayed near 31% to 32%.
- Most of the drop came after 2015-19, when the share was 21.5%.
- Charts: `01-share-within-reach.png`, `02-rentals-and-within-reach.png`.

**3. The reason: rent rose 32% after inflation, and renter income rose 8%.**
- Among the 15 Atlanta-area counties with at least 10,000 renter households,
  the gap between rent growth and renter income growth was widest in Gwinnett.
  Clayton, DeKalb and Cherokee are close behind, within the survey's margins.
- It is not a story of a few rich homeowners and many poor renters. Gwinnett's
  renter median is 55% of its owner median, about the national figure.
- Strong home prices are part of it, but Cobb had the same rise in home prices
  (83% against Gwinnett's 82%) with renter income keeping up better.
- Chart: `09-income-vs-rent.png`.

**4. On the rent side, this is not a story of high-priced new apartments. Rents
in older rentals rose faster than renters' income.**
- Apartments built since 2010 are about 10% of Gwinnett's rentals. Their median
  rent ($1,870) is 3% above the county median ($1,810).
- Older apartments, built before 2000, held about the same number of renters
  in both periods. The number within reach fell from about 9,600 to about
  4,100.
- Gwinnett built few homes for the people who arrived: 0.65 permitted units per
  added household, lower than every large neighbor but Clayton.
- Older houses became rentals: houses went from 31% to 39.5% of rentals, and
  their rents run above the county median.
- Charts: `05-rent-by-year-built.png`, `04-older-apartments.png`,
  `07-permits-per-household.png`.

**5. On the income side, renters' pay lagged Cobb, DeKalb and Fulton.**
- Pay for Gwinnett renters who worked full time all year fell 3% after
  inflation (within about 5 points). It rose 7% in Cobb, 10% in DeKalb and 21%
  in Fulton.
- It is not that fewer renters were working. Workers per renter household rose
  from 1.29 to 1.36, the same rise as in Cobb.
- Pay of jobs located in Gwinnett rose 1% after inflation, the smallest rise of
  the eight counties.
- Caveat: renter pay also fell in Henry, Cherokee and Clayton. Gwinnett stands
  out because its rents rose like Cobb's while its pay moved like Henry's.
- Chart: `11-renter-pay.png`.
