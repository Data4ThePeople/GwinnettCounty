## LinkedIn

Media: `images/01-atlanta-map.png`. The map shows the finding at a glance, where the hero is a street scene that needs the text to explain it. The text is Eric's intro and the email's second part, shortened, with the last line pointing to the first comment.

```
Last week, we dug into our Rentals Within Reach visualization and pulled out what looked like one of the most affordable counties in America for renters: Dawson County, Nebraska.

Today, we went hunting on the opposite side of the ledger – the counties that were the least affordable for renters. Three of the ten least affordable counties outside college counties were in the Atlanta metro area. But one county stood out from the rest – Gwinnett County, situated northeast of downtown Atlanta.

Gwinnett County had just 10.5% of rental units “within reach” of the median renter household income, the lowest of the 1,922 counties we ranked, leaving out college counties. In 2005-2009, it was 25.5%. After inflation, its median rent rose 32% while its median renter household income rose 8%.

Here is the twist. Gwinnett added about 41,000 rentals over this period, yet it lost about 5,500 rentals within reach. And the pay of renters who worked full time all year showed no measurable rise.

Gwinnett is an extreme example, but rents are outpacing renter incomes in other places too. When rents outpace renter incomes year after year, it can work like a gravitational pull, widening the gap between the economic prospects of renters and homeowners and putting the American Dream further out of reach for renters.

We need to do better. The full report is linked in the first comment.
```
