# [Eric: title]

Last week, we dug into our [Rentals Within Reach](https://www.data4thepeople.com/p/rentals-within-reach) visualization and pulled out what looked like one of the most affordable counties in America for renters. That was [Dawson County, Nebraska](https://www.data4thepeople.com/p/lexington-tyson-plant-closure), and while it’s true that it used to be affordable, that all changed earlier this year when its largest employer – the Tyson meatpacking plant – shut its doors. 

Today, we went hunting on the opposite side of the ledger – the counties that were the least affordable for renters. Interestingly, three of the ten least affordable counties outside college counties were in the Atlanta metro area. But one county stood out from the rest – Gwinnett County, situated northeast of downtown Atlanta. Gwinnett County had just 10.5% of rental units “within reach” of the median renter household income. We define within reach as a rent of no more than 30% of the median renter household’s after-tax income. 

![Map of the 29 counties of the Atlanta metro area, shaded red by the share of rentals within reach in 2020-2024, brighter for smaller shares. Gwinnett County, northeast of downtown Atlanta and outlined, is lowest at 10.5%. Clayton is 11.0%, DeKalb 14.0%, Henry 18.2%, Cobb 23.9% and Fulton 24.6%. Outer counties run higher, up to 70.0% in Haralson County.](images/01-atlanta-map.png)
*Source: Data 4 The People, Rentals Within Reach, from Census Bureau American Community Survey 5-year estimates, 2020-2024. County lines: Census Bureau, 2024.*

## The lowest share in the country, outside college counties

Nationally, Gwinnett's 10.5% was the lowest of the 1,922 counties we ranked, leaving out college counties. We ranked counties with at least 1,000 rentals and reliable survey estimates.

![Bar chart of the 10 counties with the lowest share of rentals within reach in 2020-2024, leaving out college counties. Gwinnett County, Georgia, is lowest at 10.5%, followed by Clayton County, Georgia, at 11.0%, Lincoln County, New Mexico, at 11.8% and DeKalb County, Georgia, at 14.0%. The rest range from 14.2% to 15.4%.](images/02-least-affordable.png)
*Source: Data 4 The People, Rentals Within Reach, from Census Bureau American Community Survey 5-year estimates, 2020-2024.*

Two caveats. Clayton County is only half a point behind. And with college counties included, Gwinnett ranks third, behind Whitman County, Washington, and Benton County, Oregon.

## A 59% decline

In 2005-2009, 25.5% of Gwinnett's rentals were within reach of its median renter household. By 2020-2024, 10.5% were. The national share was 32% at the start and 31% at the end. Gwinnett added rentals over those years, but the number within reach still fell.

![Stacked bar chart of Gwinnett County rentals in four periods. All rentals grew from 65,800 in 2005-2009 to 85,500, 97,300 and 107,200 in 2020-2024. Rentals within reach were 16,800 (25.5%), then 15,500 (18.1%), then 20,900 (21.5%), then 11,300 (10.5%).](images/03-rentals-within-reach.png)
*Source: Data 4 The People, Rentals Within Reach, from Census Bureau American Community Survey 5-year estimates. Rentals within reach are the tool's share times its count of rentals.*

## Rent rose 32%. Renter income rose 8%.

After inflation, Gwinnett's median rent rose 32% from 2005-2009 to 2020-2024, while its median renter household income rose 8%. Among the 15 Atlanta-area counties with at least 10,000 renter households, Gwinnett's gap between the two was one of the widest.

![Scatter plot of 15 Atlanta-area counties, with the change in median renter income across and the change in median rent up, 2005-2009 to 2020-2024, after inflation. Gwinnett, at income up 8% and rent up 32%, sits farthest above the line where the two rose at the same rate. Clayton, DeKalb and Cherokee are close behind it.](images/04-income-vs-rent.png)
*Source: Census Bureau, American Community Survey 5-year estimates; BLS consumer price index. Gwinnett's income change has a margin of about 5 points either way.*

Home prices rose fast in Gwinnett, but they rose about as fast in Cobb County, where renters' income rose 18%.

## New rentals came in out of reach

Gwinnett added about 41,000 rentals over this period. Two kinds account for more than half of them: about 11,300 more rentals in older houses, built before 2000, and about 11,000 apartments built since 2010. Houses built since 2010 added about 6,700 more. The typical rental in each of the three cost more than the median renter household could afford: about $1,250 a month, 30% of its after-tax income. In 2020-2024, older houses rented for a median of about $1,990 and new apartments for $1,870. About 10% of the older houses and 8% of the new apartments were within reach. Houses built since 2010 rented for a median of about $2,500, and 2% were within reach.

![Bar chart of median monthly rent in Gwinnett County in 2020-2024, against the $1,250 the median renter household could afford. Houses built before 2000 $1,990, with 10% within reach. Apartments built 2010 or later $1,870, with 8% within reach. Houses built 2010 or later $2,500, with 2% within reach.](images/05-new-to-market-rents.png)
*Source: Census Bureau, American Community Survey 5-year microdata. Rents include utilities.*

That does not account for the drop in rentals within reach. Most of that drop is in older apartments, built before 2000. Gwinnett had about the same number of them in both periods, but their median rent went from about $860 to $1,660, up 92%. After inflation, that is a 31% rise, while the amount the median renter household could afford rose 8%. In 2005-2009, the median older apartment rented for about $100 more than that amount. By 2020-2024, it was about $400 more.

![Bar chart of Gwinnett County amounts in 2024 dollars. In 2005-2009, the median rent for apartments built before 2000 was $1,260, and the median renter household could afford $1,160. In 2020-2024, the median rent was $1,660, and the household could afford $1,250.](images/06-older-apartment-rents.png)
*Source: Census Bureau, American Community Survey 5-year microdata; Data 4 The People, Rentals Within Reach. Amounts in 2024 dollars.*

In other words, most of the rentals added cost more than the median renter household could afford, and the older apartments that used to be close to reach rose far faster than renters' income.

## Renters' pay did not keep up

Why did renter income not keep up? For the same kind of renter household (the same number of workers, and a householder of the same age and education), income fell about 3% after inflation. Renter households as a whole earned more mostly because the households changed: more members worked, more worked full time, and more householders were older or had college degrees. Pay itself showed no measurable rise. After inflation, the median renter who worked full time all year earned about $46,100 in 2005-2009 and $44,600 in 2020-2024. In Cobb, that pay rose 7%.

Few counties with rents rising this fast saw that. We looked at 37 counties in large metro areas where rent rose 25% or more after inflation and the survey can measure the county on its own. Only two showed a drop in full-time renter pay: Gwinnett, and Cherokee County, also in metro Atlanta. Both drops are within the survey's margin of error, Cherokee's by a wide amount. In the middle county of the group, it rose 15%.

![Bar chart of the change in median earnings of renters who worked full time all year, 2005-2009 to 2020-2024, after inflation, in 37 counties in large metro areas where median rent rose 25% or more after inflation. San Francisco is highest at 44.5%. The middle county rose about 15%. Cobb rose 6.7%, DeKalb 9.9% and Fulton 21.2%. Only Gwinnett, down 3.3%, and Cherokee, down 6.0%, show a drop. Lines on each bar show the margin of error, widest for Cherokee.](images/07-renter-pay.png)
*Source: Census Bureau, American Community Survey 5-year microdata; BLS consumer price index. Lines show 90% margins: about 5 points either way for Gwinnett and 11 for Cherokee, so the order near the bottom is not certain. Another 44 counties with rent up 25% or more share a microdata area with a neighbor and could not be measured on their own.*

## “Hotlanta” - for rents, not renter incomes

We really tried hard to keep this analysis focused on Gwinnett County. With the lowest percent of rentals within reach across its cohort of larger U.S. counties, it deserved the attention. But notice how all throughout this analysis, other neighboring Atlanta area counties kept making appearances on the charts. The analysis does not support one neat and tidy cause and effect conclusion encompassing the entire Atlanta metro area, but the data does tell us something is going on around this city that is making many areas uniquely difficult for renters. Rental prices are rising like one would expect in Hotlanta, but the incomes aren’t climbing with it. That’s why Gwinnett took pole position in this analysis – it had the largest gap between the change in rental prices and renter incomes. But is Gwinnett unique, or is it just where this perfect storm came together around Atlanta? We don’t know… but would love to hear from residents to help fill in the experiential gaps to which the data is blind.

Our other takeaway from this analysis was how different it was from Dawson County’s story. Dawson County was all about concentration risk of a small county in one large employer. It was an idiosyncratic story on the surface, but on reflection is just the manifestation of a risk facing many small counties across this country. Gwinnett County, from what we can tell, is simply a math story. It’s a story that is happening everywhere to some degree - inflation creates winners and losers. If you owned your home over the past twenty years in Gwinnett County, you are a winner from housing inflation. If you needed to rent, you are a loser from housing inflation. Yes, you lost especially hard in Gwinnett, but this story just illustrates that what matters is the ratio between your income and your expenses. We all know this right? It couldn’t be simpler. And yet, what happened in Gwinnett is happening across the country to varying degrees. In my view, this is very sad. We were all renters at one point in our life. And if we were lucky, we eventually became homeowners. But now we have counties where rental prices are accelerating far faster than incomes. How are renters in Gwinnett supposed to save for a house if they must spend 43% of their after-tax income on a median rental?

Our job is the tools and analysis to help you understand the data. It’s not policy. But geez this is depressing for those seeking the American Dream (without wealth parents to finance it for them). Hopefully some smart minds come together to first acknowledge this problem, and then enact solutions to give people some hope for a better match between wages and affordable rentals.

## What this does not tell you

- **Why.** These are patterns that came alongside the rise in rents. The data does not show what caused them.
- **Survey estimates.** Every number comes from a survey and has a margin of error. Gwinnett's lead over Clayton, and over the next counties in the Atlanta comparison, is small enough that the order could change.
- **One way to measure affordability.** "Within reach" uses the tool's default: the median renter household, after taxes, paying 30% of income. Other settings give other rankings.
- **Different people.** The survey takes two snapshots of different renters. It does not follow the same people over time.
- **Year built.** Households report when their building was built, and some may not know exactly.

## How we did this

- **Shares within reach** come from our Rentals Within Reach tool, built from Census Bureau American Community Survey 5-year estimates for 2005-2009, 2010-2014, 2015-2019 and 2020-2024. Counties with fewer than 1,000 rentals, or with unreliable estimates, are left out. College counties are the tool's 71 counties where 15% or more of residents age 3 and over are enrolled in college.
- **Rent and income changes** use the published median gross rent and median renter household income, put in 2024 dollars with the consumer price index.
- **Rentals by building type and year built, rents of older apartments, and renter pay,** come from the survey's microdata (individual household records) for Gwinnett and six neighboring counties. Forsyth County is left out of these because its 2005-2009 records also cover Dawson and Pickens counties. Full time means 35 or more hours a week for 50 or more weeks a year.
- **Home prices** come from the Federal Housing Finance Agency's county house price index.
- An independent check recomputed every number from the raw data. The code and data notes are on [GitHub](https://github.com/Data4ThePeople/GwinnettCounty).
